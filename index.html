What I took · Psychoanalysis

Freud’s theory of Eros and Thanatos

Thanatos driving Blanche to Eros. “The grim reaper had put up his tent on our doorstep” The psychological weight of the death of her family, is what brought Blanche towards self-destructive behavior

Freud, Beyond the Pleasure Principle, 1920

In Williams’s life · Frank Merlo

Merlo, his partner from 1948 to 1963, gave him “a stable long-term relationship”; after Merlo died, “a lot of problems with substance abuse”.

What I took · Psychoanalysis

The gender conformity, characterization, and portrayal of the characters as the id, ego, and superego:

  • Stanley is used as a character to portray all males, who is also a portrayal of the id is rewarded. It is excused under the guise of male virility and his violent behavior carry no social consequence, rather enforces his authority as the master of the household
  • Blanche is shown as the superego through her characterization of the “Ideal woman”, made to satisfy patriarchal expectations. Which leads to her delusions as she cannot perform this act of being chaste without being in continuous conflict with her own sexual desires.

What I took · Symbolism

Tragedy is not something that has been directly portrayed in the first 3 scenes, rather, it is symbolized along with the effect of it.

Stop 4 · Comparison with Persepolis · 1:15 in the talk

Streetcar and Persepolis

Three comparisons. Each one sets a Streetcar technique against a Persepolis technique, then says what is similar, what is different and why it matters.

1. Repression: inside or imposed

Streetcar

Technique: a flat split by a curtain

  • “Nothing belongs on a poker table but cards, chips and whiskey.”

    Stanley, Scene 3

  • “I can’t stand a naked light bulb…”

    Blanche, Scene 3: her censor is inside her

Persepolis

Technique: a narrator who names the repression

  • “Naturally, the regime became more repressive.”

    p. 117

  • “Without them it wouldn’t be psychologically bearable.”

    the secret parties, p. 106

Similar

Both party nights hide cards, drink and music behind curtains.

Different

Blanche’s censor is her own superego; in Tehran the censor is the state.

Why it matters

Satrapi’s family resists outside repression to stay sane. Blanche’s inner superego is breaking her.

2. Who is blamed for desire?

Streetcar

Technique: stage directions and stage light

  • He “sizes women up at a glance, with sexual classifications”

    Stanley, Scene 1

  • “I won’t be looked at in this merciless glare!”

    Blanche, Scene 1

Persepolis

Technique: one TV panel and a reaction

  • “Women’s hair emanates rays that excite men.”

    on TV, p. 74

  • “Incredible! They think all men are perverts!!”

    Marji’s father, p. 74

Similar

Men’s desire is treated as an id nobody can stop, so women do the covering.

Different

Tehran makes the veil law, and Marji’s father mocks it. Blanche covers herself.

Why it matters

Freud calls it projection: blaming someone else for your own forbidden wish.

3. Trauma: repeating or working through

Streetcar

Technique: offstage music (the polka)

  • “The music of the polka rises up, faint in the distance.”

    stage direction, Scene 1

  • “The boy—the boy died.”

    Blanche, Scene 1

Persepolis

Technique: the all-black panel

  • “No scream in the world could have relieved my suffering and my anger.”

    p. 142

  • “I had learned that you should always shout louder than your aggressor.”

    p. 143

Similar

Both texts break off where words fail.

Different

Blanche relives her loss through the polka; Satrapi draws hers and fights back.

Why it matters

Freud: we repeat “not as a memory but as an action”. Persepolis is the working through.

    Glossary

    Each term in plain words, with the moment in the play that shows it.

    Id Stanley
    The part of the mind that wants pleasure now.
    “Animal joy in his being is implicit in all his movements and attitudes.” (Scene 1)
    Ego Stella
    The part that deals with reality and tries to balance the id and the superego. Freud pictures a rider holding back a stronger horse.
    Stella answers back, then goes back to Stanley.
    Superego Blanche
    Society’s rules, taken inside as a conscience.
    “It’s the man who is the id, and the woman is forced to be the superego.” (Lecture 2 · 25:51)
    Repression
    Pushing a painful wish or memory out of awareness. It comes back in other forms.
    “…this repression… is what causes us to experience neurotic symptoms of mental illness.” (Lecture 1 · 57:10)
    Denial
    Refusing to admit something that is true.
    Blanche drinks in secret, then says “No, I—rarely touch it.” (Scene 1)
    Projection
    Blaming someone else for your own forbidden wish.
    Mitch watches Blanche, then says “Poker should not be played in a house with women.” (Scene 3)
    Regression
    Falling back into childish behaviour under stress.
    Stanley sobs “My baby doll’s left me!” (Scene 3)
    Illusion
    What Blanche puts between herself and reality: the lantern, the fibs and the stories.
    “…a woman’s charm is fifty percent illusion…” (Scene 2)
    Eros and Thanatos
    The drive toward life and love, and the drive toward death (Freud, 1920).
    “Why, the Grim Reaper had put up his tent on our doorstep!” (Scene 1)
    Mirror stage
    Lacan’s idea that we build a sense of self by identifying with an image of ourselves (1949).
    Blanche’s clothes, stories and dim light build “another perfect ‘Blanche’”.
    Persona
    Jung’s word for the social mask.
    “the self we put on in the social world to be socially acceptable” (Lecture 2 · 42:02)
    Repeating and working through
    What we cannot remember, we repeat as action. Working through means facing it (Freud, 1914).
    The polka keeps returning for Blanche (Scene 1).
